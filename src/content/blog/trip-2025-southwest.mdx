This post will cover stops 14 through 19 as I explore the American Southwest.

## The Grand Canyon

I think the Grand Canyon is the opposite of Mount Rushmore.

Mount Rushmore is a monument that fits within a frame.
If you've seen a picture of Mount Rushmore, you basically get the gist.
It's hand-carved slopes transform a mountianside from natural beauty to cultural icon.

The word "Grand" doesn't do the Grand Canyon justice.
There's a sheer scale to it that makes observation akin to astronomy:
a reminder of how miniscule a human really is.
No one picture does it justice.


As someone who recently underestimated a hike, I appreciated that the signage emphasizes this scale, and makes the difficulty of the full hike clear.

<Lightbox collection="trip-2025" image="20250609_143421_IMG_1595.avif" alt="Sign warning not to hike too far at the Grand Canyon" />

I know there's tourist attractions at the Grand Canyon, where they'll bus you into the most picturesque spots, or let you walk on [a glass skywalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon_Skywalk).
However, I decided to keep it simple, and just wander at my own pace.
The [Grand Canyon Shuttle](https://www.nps.gov/grca/planyourvisit/shuttle-buses.htm)
made it easy to explore on a whim.

I loved seeing the mules at work in the Grand Canyon.
They do a few jobs.
For one, you can book a mule ride for a casual $185.56/person[^mule].
They also carry equipment and supplies to the canyon floor, which is what I believe is happening here.
In this case, I believe they're coming back from the 7.5 mile route[^mile] across the South Kaibab Trail to get to Phantom Ranch,
which is the only lodging below the canyon rim.

[^mule]: Source: https://www.grandcanyonlodges.com/plan/mule-rides/
[^mile]: Source: https://www.theadventurediet.com/blog/a-complete-guide-on-how-to-visit-phantom-ranch-grand-canyons-most-epic-place-to-stay

Also, I want to highlight the incredibly solid trail construction.
This is another example of the outstanding work by the CCC, and continues the theme of durability we talked about earlier:
it's lasted ~100 years, and it's likely to last at least another 100 years with light maintenance.

It's easy to lose sight of how incredibly massive it is, compared to humans.

Eleven Native-American tribes, including the Basketmaker-Pueblo, Hopi, Hualapai, and Yavapai-Apache,
have a history of living in or near the Grand Canyon.
[The history is fascinating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Grand_Canyon_area#Early_history),
though I don't feel qualified to explain it here.
Nonetheless, as I see how small humans are compared to the canyon paths,
I also reflect on how impressive it is that humans lived
in the canyon thousands of years before there were tourist-friendly trails.

## Zion

I enjoy hiking and exploring nature as a way to get away from
the hustle and bustle of urban life.
However, most city streets are less densely packed than Zion national park.
Let's take a look at the numbers.


|             | Miles of Trails                    | Annual Visitors (2024) <sup>[source][stats]</sup>| Visitors/Mile |
|:------------|-----------------------------------:|:------------------------------------------------:|--------------:|
| Zion        | 90 <sup>[source][zion]</sup>       | 4,946,592                                        |    **54,962** |
| Yosemite    | 750 <sup>[source][yose]</sup>      | 4,121,807                                        |     **5,496** |
| Yellowstone | 1,100 <sup>[source][yell]</sup>    | 4,744,353                                        |     **4,313** |

[zion]: https://www.nps.gov/zion/planyourvisit/backpacking.htm
[yell]: https://www.nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/trails.htm
[yose]: https://www.nps.gov/yose/planyourvisit/trails.htm
[stats]: https://irma.nps.gov/Stats/

Obviously, it's naïve to assume that visitors are equally spread out over all trails, but the point is in the proportions.
Zion recieves an *order of magnitude* more visitors per mile of trail than other parks with similar annual attendance.

## Bryce Canyon

Sitting at around half the attendance of the parks we last discussed, Bryce Canyon received 2,498,075 visitors in 2024,
making it relatively underrated in my view.
It has a fast and efficient shuttle service, and plenty of unique and interesting hiking trails to explore.

There was [a quote on a sign](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/the-legend-people-wayside.htm) about the

that stuck out to me:

<QuoteBlock attribution="NPS Sign" url="https://www.nps.gov/places/000/the-legend-people-wayside.htm">
The Paiutes inhabited this region for hundreds of years before the arrival of European Americans. A sacred oral tradition of the Paiute Indians states that the hoodoos are ancient legend people turned into stone by coyote as a punishment for bad deeds. It is the custom of the Paiutes to tell these stories only during the winter season. Spring summer and fall are for hunting gathering and storing food it is out of respect for this custom that the authentic story is not repeated here.

I liked this for two reasons.
For one,
I can totally see how some freaky-looking hoodoos got personified as people turned to stone.
Additionally, I generally think of written text as being outside of time, so it's interesting to
see a reference to a story that should not be printed, lest it be read outside of the appropriate season.
Very interesting!

## Warner Campground

Early on, when planning my trip, I decided that
I wanted to plan a basic structure, but leave opportunity for spontaneity.
Rather than having every minute of the day planned out,
I'd have a general plan of where I need to be, but
I'd always reserve agency to decide what exactly to do on the day.

I think this worked out well overall!

However, there were several situations where I had to make a change of plans.
For example, I planned to visit Arches National Park.
When planning, I noticed the nearest campsite that wasn't completely booked was an hour away,
which seemed unfortunate but doable.
I noted that it was only accessible via gravel roads, but I decided that I'd figure it out.


<Lightbox collection="trip-2025" image="20250617_092412_IMG_2936.avif" alt="photo of gravel road leading to Warner Campground" />

However, when I arrived, I felt that the gravel and dirt roads were taxing
enough on my car that it'd be wise to minimize the times I drove on them.
As such, I ended up skipping Arches, and just exploring the the mountain my campsite was on.
Sure, it wasn't what I planned, but I ended up really enjoying it anyway!

## Black Canyon of the Gunnison

Now, by this point I'd visited the canyons at Yellowstone, 
Yosemite, the Grand Canyon, and Zion. 
So, when visiting the Black Canyon of the Gunnison, I felt an almost paternal air towards it:
"Who's a good little canyon? Are you gonna grow up to be like the Grand Canyon one day?"

It's certainly a nice place, but I think it helps 
contextualise how utterly amazing all of the other national parks are by comparison.
As far as I'm aware, there's around 19 miles of trails in the 
park,<sup>[†](https://www.nps.gov/blca/planyourvisit/hikingtrails.htm)</sup>
making Zion look massive in comparison.
